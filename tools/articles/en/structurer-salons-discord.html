A server's structure is its first impression. Within seconds, a new member should understand where they are, what they can do and where to talk. Here is the method I use when I structure or restructure a server.

Think journey, not list

Before creating a single channel, ask yourself one question: what should a member do during their first five minutes? Usually: discover the server, read the rules, pick their roles, then join the conversation. Your structure should follow that journey, from top to bottom.

The essential categories

  • Welcome: rules, announcements, role selection.
  • Community: general chat, introductions, media sharing.
  • Events: event announcements, contests, results.
  • Support: questions, tickets, suggestions.
  • Voice: a few voice channels, no more than you need.

Depending on your project, add a dedicated category (games, creations, bots…), but keep this foundation.

Clear, consistent names

A good channel name makes sense without clicking on it. Pick a format and stick to it: for example an emoji followed by a short name, such as “💬 | chat” or “📢 | announcements”. The emoji helps people spot channels at a glance, as long as you keep it simple.

Fewer, but better

An empty channel does more harm than a missing one. Merge quiet channels, archive the ones nobody uses any more, and only create a new channel when there is a real need.

Permissions by category

Set permissions at category level rather than channel by channel: it is clearer and safer. Information channels stay read-only, staff channels are invisible to members, and newcomers only see the essentials until they have accepted the rules.
